Say it in your own words

One instruction appears in almost every 0500 question. It is also the instruction second-language candidates lose the most marks on. Today you learn to obey it.

About 15 minutes One skill Paper 1 Q1, Q2, Q3, Q4 · Paper 2 Q1(b)

Why this is lesson one

Cambridge does not ask you to copy. It asks you to transform. The phrase “in your own words” appears in Question 1(b), Question 2(a), Question 3(b) and Question 4 of Paper 1, and in Question 1(b) of Paper 2. That is most of the reading marks on both papers.

And the mark schemes are blunt about what happens if you lift:

From the 2027 specimen mark scheme

“Answers which are entirely in the words of the text should not be credited.” — Paper 1, Question 1(e)(ii)

“Includes lifted sections.” — Paper 1, Question 2(a), Table B, Writing, Level 1

This is not a small deduction. Lifting can take a question to zero. And if English is not your first language, lifting is the natural thing to do — the words are right there, and they are correct. The skill is to stop trusting them.

The rule that unlocks 2-mark questions

Look at how the examiner actually awards marks. This is the real mark scheme for a 2-mark item on the specimen paper:

1(b)(i) Use your own words to explain what the text means by: ‘forest dweller’.

Award 2 marks for full explanation (both strands). Award 1 mark for partial explanation.

• an area with trees / a jungle / wooded place
• living in / inhabited / inhabitant / occupier / reside in

0500/01 Specimen mark scheme, for examination from 2027

Two marks, two strands. The phrase “forest dweller” contains two ideas, and you get one mark for each:

“forest dweller” — 2 marks, 2 strands
StrandWhat it meansAccepted in your own words
1a wooded placean area with trees · a jungle · woodland
2living therelives in · inhabits · resides in · occupies

If you write “a forest dweller”, you have given neither strand in your own words, so you score nothing. If you write “a creature that lives in a wooded area”, you have both, and you score 2.

The rule

Marks = strands. A 2-mark “own words” question wants two ideas. A 3-mark question wants three. Before you write, count the ideas hiding inside the phrase.

Three moves that do the work

  1. Change the word class. “Dweller” is a noun; “lives” is a verb. “Resembled” is a verb; “looked like” is a verb phrase. Changing the grammar forces new words.
  2. Swap the concept, keep the meaning. “Forest” → “wooded area”. “Faintly” → “barely”. “Exploits” → “adventures”. Same idea, different word.
  3. Compress. Drop decoration, keep the core. “Sniffing around for a juicy headline” → “hunting for a scandal”.

Worked example

“Also small, a forest dweller and eater of leaves, this ancient ancestor only faintly resembled the horse we know today…”

Text A, 0500/01 Specimen insert

The mark scheme accepts:

  • hardly / scarcely / barely / just weakly / just slightly / not much — strand one
  • (looked) like / appeared similar (to) — strand two

So a full-mark answer is: “it looked only slightly like the modern horse.” Two strands, no lifted words, meaning intact.

2. “only faintly resembled” — 2 marks

Two strands. How strong is the similarity, and what is being compared?

0 words
Accepted answers

“it looked only slightly like the modern horse” — strand one: only slightly; strand two: looked like.

The mark scheme accepts: hardly / scarcely / barely / just weakly / just slightly / not much + (looked) like / appeared similar (to).

3. “sniffing around for a juicy headline” — 2 marks

Harder. One strand is the searching; the other is what is being searched for.

0 words
Accepted answers

“hunting for a scandal that would sell newspapers” — strand one: hunting / searching; strand two: scandal that sells papers.

The mark scheme's own gloss: “like a wild animal or dog searching for a scent of something tasty to eat; hunting for scandal.”

What this is worth

On Paper 1, “own words” questions carry roughly 25–30 of the 80 marks. On Paper 2, Question 1(b) is 10 reading marks and 25 writing marks, and lifting is penalised in both. Getting this one habit right is the difference between a C and an A.
