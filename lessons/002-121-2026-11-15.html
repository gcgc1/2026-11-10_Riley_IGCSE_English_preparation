Marks = strands

Lesson 1 gave you the rule. This one tells you when it applies — and when counting strands is exactly the wrong thing to do.

About 15 minutes One skill Paper 1 Q1, Q2(b), Q3 · Paper 2 Q1(b)

Why this matters

Some of the mark scheme is arithmetic. Look at the instructions the examiner follows for Question 1:

“Award 1 mark for each idea, up to a maximum of 3.”

“Award 2 marks for full explanation (both strands). Award 1 mark for partial explanation.”

0500/01 Specimen mark scheme, Question 1(e)(i) and 1(b)(i)

That is not a vague instruction to “write about” something. It is a count. Three ideas, three marks. One idea, one mark. And if you repeat an idea, the mark scheme is explicit about what happens:

Generic Marking Principle, English & Media

“DO NOT give further credit for what is effectively repetition of a correct point already credited… This applies equally to ‘mirror statements’.”

So the single most useful thing you can do before writing an answer is decide how many ideas this question is buying — and how many you actually have.

But only half the paper works this way

Here is the trap. Counting strands is a real skill, and applying it to the wrong question will hurt you.

Three shapes of counting question

Not all counts are the same. Read the question closely to see which shape you have:

  1. N separate facts. “Give two of the main ways in which horses were used by humans in the past.” Two marks, two different facts. Award 1 mark for each idea, up to maximum of 2.
  2. N strands of one phrase. “Use your own words to explain what the text means by ‘forest dweller’.” Two marks, but only one phrase — split into two ideas: the wooded place, and the living there.
  3. N separate words. “Use your own words to explain what the writer means by each of the words in bold.” One mark per word. Three words, three marks.

Worked example

1(e)(i) Explain why scientists first thought Botai was ‘the most likely place’ that horses were first tamed by humans.

Award 1 mark for each idea, up to a maximum of 3.

• enormous collections of horse remains
• ceramic remains suggested diet included horse milk
• evidence of damaged teeth suggested horses wore mouthpieces

0500/01 Specimen mark scheme

Three marks, three ideas. Now look at what different answers score:

The same question, three answers
AnswerMarksWhy
“They found a huge number of horse bones at the site.” 1 One idea. Accurate, but it stops at one.
“They found a huge number of horse bones — 300,000 fragments — which is an enormous quantity of remains from one place, far more than you would expect.” 1 Still one idea, just longer. Detail and length do not create new strands.
“Huge numbers of horse bones were found; pottery showed people drank mare’s milk; and some teeth were damaged in a way that suggests mouthpieces.” 3 Three distinct ideas, each one creditworthy.
The rule

Length is not marks. Distinct ideas are marks. A 3-mark answer is three short ideas, not one long one.

Two rules that stop you losing marks

Do not repeat yourself

“Horses were not genetically linked to modern horses, meaning they were a different species” is one idea, not two. The mark scheme calls the second half a “mirror statement” and refuses to credit it twice.

Do not over-write a 1-mark item

A 1-mark question wants a word or a short phrase. Writing three sentences for it costs you time you need for Question 3(d) — which is worth ten marks for 250 words. Marking is positive, so extra answers are never penalised; but time is the real currency.

1. “What is the other name for an Eohippus, according to paragraph 1?” — 1 mark

Counting. How many ideas? How long should the answer be?

Accepted answer

“dawn horse” — that is the whole answer. One mark, two words.

If you wrote a sentence, you were not wrong — but you spent time you needed later. A 1-mark item wants a word or phrase.

2. “Explain why scientists first thought Botai was ‘the most likely place’ that horses were first tamed.” — 3 marks

Counting. Three distinct ideas. Do not develop any of them at length.

0 words
Accepted answers — 1 mark each

• enormous collections of horse remains
• ceramic remains suggested the diet included horse milk
• evidence of damaged teeth suggested horses wore mouthpieces

Notice the shape: three short clauses, three marks. If your three sentences all describe the bones, you have one idea, not three.

3. “Assess the writer’s attitude towards the cloning of endangered animals. Use three details from Text B.” — 5 marks

Banding, not counting. There is no number of ideas that earns 5. What does the band 3 descriptor ask for?

0 words
What level 3 requires

“Clear understanding of the writer’s attitude with relevant support from the text.”

So: name the attitude precisely (not “positive” — cautiously admiring, frustrated that the technology is underused), then support it with three specific details. The three details are evidence, not three separate marks.

This is the difference the whole lesson turns on: counting questions reward number; banding questions reward precision and support.

What this is worth

Question 1 on Paper 1 is 20 marks, and every one of them is point-marked. Question 3(a) and 3(b) add 5 more. So 25 of Paper 1's 80 marks turn on whether you can count. Getting this right is the cheapest 25 marks on the paper.
