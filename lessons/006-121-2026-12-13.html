Attitude: what the writer feels

The mark scheme never asks whether a writer is positive or negative. It asks for the attitude — and it asks for it precisely.

About 15 minutes One skill Paper 1 Q2(b) · Paper 2 Q1(a)

Why this, now

Two questions on the two papers ask the same thing in almost the same words. Paper 1, Question 2(b): “Assess the writer’s attitude towards the cloning of endangered animals.” Paper 2, Question 1(a): “Use your own words to evaluate Jaz’s attitude to work.”

Both are marked by the same band descriptor. Here it is, for Paper 2:

Level 3 (4–5 marks) Clear understanding of Jaz’s attitude with relevant support from the text.

Level 2 (2–3 marks) Some understanding of Jaz’s attitude with some supporting detail.

Level 1 (1 mark) Limited understanding of Jaz’s attitude.

0500/02 Specimen mark scheme, Table A (Reading)

Read the three levels again. Nothing in them mentions the number of details, the length of the answer, or whether you used quotations. The whole scale turns on one thing: how clearly you understand the attitude. So the first job — this lesson — is to name it exactly. Next week you supply the proof.

The knowledge

An attitude is a stance towards something. That gives the question two halves, and a strong answer always contains both:

Two halves of every attitude answer
HalfQuestion it answersExample
The feelingWhat is the stance?Competitive
The objectTowards what?Towards her work and her colleagues

“She is positive” fails both halves: it names no feeling precisely and no object. “She is fiercely competitive about her work” passes both — and it is already worth more than a vague answer with three quotations attached.

The word that costs marks

Positive and negative are not attitudes. They are directions. Every text in the exam has one of them, so they distinguish nothing — which is why they sit at Level 1.

Attitudes are rarely single. Real writers hold two at once — admiration and frustration, pride and resentment. When that happens, the precise answer names the pair. The mark scheme is built for this: it lists several possible attitudes for the same text, because a good answer may choose any of them, and a very good answer may hold two together.

Worked example

Paper 2, Question 1(a). Read Jaz's own words and look for the stance underneath them.

‘I’d worked for months to cultivate one prospective client,’ explains Jaz. ‘Everyone knew winning a big order from that client could be a deciding factor in which of us from the international sales team got promoted. Then I discover from a junior colleague that Ravi’s also trying to net this wealthy individual, even though Ravi knew I was already in pursuit! I knew if I ignored the situation, he’d just seal the deal, profiting shamelessly from all my slaving away, and our boss would promote Ravi. But if I’d directly confronted Ravi, it could’ve made me look petty and selfish. At the time, I calculated the best way to fight back was to be just as devious as Ravi.’

The mark scheme lists five attitudes the extract supports. Any of them is creditable — this is not a guessing game with one right answer:

The mark scheme's own list
AttitudeDetail it rests on
Competitivewinning a big order · I was already in pursuit · trying to net

Notice that these are not five different answers. They are five facets of one person, and the strongest response joins two of them into a single stance:

A Level 3 first sentence

“Jaz is fiercely competitive and quietly resentful: she treats her work as a contest she has to win, and she believes a colleague is stealing the reward for her effort.”

Two feelings, one object (her work), no quotation yet. It would already be hard to place this below Level 2, and the details next week will take it to the top.

Model — a Level 3 opening

“Jaz is fiercely competitive and quietly resentful: she treats her work as a contest she has to win, and she believes a colleague is stealing the reward for her effort.”

Any two of the mark scheme's five attitudes would work here — competitive, ruthless, ambitious, hard-working, unfairly treated — provided you name them precisely and attach them to work. What would not work: “She has a positive attitude to her job.”

What this is worth

Two banded questions, ten marks between them — Paper 1 Q2(b) is 5, Paper 2 Q1(a) is 5. Both are decided in the first sentence you write. If that sentence is vague, the details that follow cannot rescue it.
