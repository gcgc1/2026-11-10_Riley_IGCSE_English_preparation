Reading and vocabulary

One book every four weeks. One sentence every Sunday. Five words every week. This page is the rule for all 121 weeks.

45 minutes, every Sunday One book per block Five words a week
Why this is not optional

Writing Objective 3 asks you to “use a range of vocabulary and sentence structures appropriate to context” — the syllabus's own words. That is half of Paper 2. It cannot be crammed, because there are no dictionaries in the exam: every word you own had to be met, used, and met again before the morning of the paper.

Reading does the second job too. R4 asks you to explain why a writer chose a word. No rule teaches that. You learn it by noticing it happen, several hundred times, in sentences nobody wrote for a textbook.

This block

Read on Sunday. One sitting, 45 minutes. The page counts below assume about 170 words a minute; if you read more slowly, read for the full 45 minutes and stop where you are.

The one rule that keeps this honest

A block is one book — unless the book is over 32,000 words, in which case it gets two blocks and the table says so, as Holes does. No pretending. If you fall behind, read to the end of the chapter on Monday morning and mark your work afterwards. Never skip pages to catch up; a book you skim teaches you nothing.

The Sunday sentence

After the reading, choose one sentence and bring it to Monday's session — paste it into the chat with your practice answers. It gets marked, briefly, alongside them. Three tests, and all three come from the Paper 1 mark scheme:

What the sentence has to do — Paper 1, Table E
TestThe mark scheme's wordsWhat fails it
1. Name the exact word or phrase“precisely selected words/phrases”Quoting a whole paragraph, or paraphrasing it
2. Say what it adds“adds meaning and associations”“It emphasises the point.” Emphasises what?
3. Say why the writer chose it“demonstrates the writer's reasons for using them”“It makes the reader want to read on.” Every sentence does that.

Here is the standard, on a real specimen sentence:

“…the invention of the spoked wheel unlocking the potential of these elegant, living machines, powered only by grass.”

0500/01 Specimen insert, for examination from 2027 — Text A, “Horses”

Model: “Living machines — ‘machines’ makes the horse sound built for a job, and ‘living’ fights it, because a machine has no will of its own. The writer puts the two words together so the reader feels the contradiction: people used horses as equipment and still called them elegant.”

One sentence of the book, three sentences of you. That is the whole task. It takes five minutes and it is the same move Q3(d) will ask you to make ten times in the exam.

The trap

Naming the device — “the writer uses a metaphor” — is a Level 2 move in Table E: “correctly identifies linguistic devices.” It is worth 3–4 marks out of 10. The marks are in the words and their effect, not in the label. Practise the sentence, not the terminology.

The five words

Five words a week, taken from the book you are reading. Not from a word list, not from a dictionary, not from this page. A word you met in a sentence you were enjoying is a word you will still have in 2029.

Write each one as a card with four fields:

The card
WordThe sentence, copied exactlyMeaning in eight words or fewerMy own sentence
tantalisingly “Tantalisingly, scientists are unable to pinpoint just one culture…” almost within reach, but still out of reach Tantalisingly, the results arrived a week after the deadline.
  • Copy the sentence exactly. A word only means something in a context — that is precisely how Q3 tests word meaning. Your card keeps the context.
  • Eight words or fewer for the meaning. If you need twenty, you have defined it without understanding it. The limit is the test.
  • Your own sentence must be about something else. Same word, different subject. Reusing the book's subject lets you copy its grammar without thinking.

Cull, retrieve, use

Collecting is not learning. 121 Sundays at five words is 605 cards, and nobody owns 605 words. So the bank is deliberately cruel:

The four-week cycle
WhenWhat happens
SundayRead. Write five cards. Choose the one sentence to bring.
MondayPaste the five cards and the sentence into the chat with your practice answers. I mark the meanings and reject vague ones.
WednesdayRetrieve: this week's quiz cold, plus five words from your bank — any week, no looking.
SaturdayWrite the 350 words. Underline three bank words you used. Wrong word, wrong place, and it comes out.
End of blockCull. Keep the best 6 of your 20. The question is not “did I learn it?” but “would I actually use it in a composition?”
Test weekIn Tuesday's marking session, five minutes: ten words drawn from your keepers, one new sentence each, marked cold.

Six kept words a block is about 180 over the course. A hundred and eighty words you can spell, place and pronounce are worth more than six hundred you once wrote down — and the cull is itself a W3 skill: deciding which word earns its place.

Keeping the bank

Write the cards in vocabulary/bank.md in this workspace, one table row each, or in a notebook if you prefer paper — the file is only a convenience. Either way the loop is the same: five cards on Sunday, pasted on Monday, retrieved on Wednesday, three of them used on Saturday.

Ask me

Send five cards even if you think two of them are weak — especially then. A wrong definition is one line of feedback and a word you will not misuse in the exam. A wrong definition you never showed anyone is a mark lost in 2029.
